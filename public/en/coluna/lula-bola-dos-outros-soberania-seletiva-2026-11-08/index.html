My ball, your field, and the state's whistle

Lula calls for electoral sovereignty while a political ally shares campaign tactics in Argentina. Cooperation and interference deserve the same standard, regardless of party.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-08

Some versions of sovereignty work like an electric fence: outsiders get shocked; friends are handed the gate remote. On September 21, 2026, Lula asked the United States not to interfere in Brazil's elections. The principle is sound: Brazilians choose their own government. But political vocabulary suddenly becomes elastic when people from the same political camp cross the border to share election tactics with their neighbors. Depending on who is traveling, the word 'interference' changes outfits before clearing immigration.

On November 2, Congressman André Janones, a key figure in Lula's digital mobilization, met Sergio Massa and Cristina Kirchner in Buenos Aires to share techniques from Brazil's 2026 campaign. He described that purpose to CNN himself. Lula helped found the São Paulo Forum, a network of left-wing parties established in 1990. But a crucial distinction keeps criticism credible: there is no established evidence that the Forum organized this trip, that Lula ordered it, or that Brazil's government paid for the meetings. When criticism needs to invent a secret chain of command, it has already weakened its strongest point.

The image that comes to mind is a sulking child who takes the ball home after losing. At least the child usually takes their own ball. In a cartoon version of interventionist politics, someone grabs the neighbor's ball, establishes the National Recreation Agency, declares the local field a strategic asset, taxes every kick, and calls the owner selfish for wanting to play. By the end, nobody has scored, but the government announces record-breaking sporting regulations. And anyone who complains is charged for a replacement whistle.

Behind the joke lies a serious question: are we applying the same standard? Political parties have long exchanged ideas across borders. Visiting a candidate, discussing communication strategies, or openly supporting an ally is not automatically unlawful state interference. Nor does a foreign leader's criticism of a government, by itself, amount to election fraud. If 'interference' means covert public funding, diplomatic coercion, or tampering with the electoral process, show the evidence. If it merely means taking part in another country's political debate, the rule should apply to friends and opponents alike. Sovereignty should not require a party-issued password.

The same habit of bending words appears in economic debates. A government office can decree that a table must be classified as a machine gun; the table still will not fire. It can rename taxes 'contributions,' spending 'investment,' and control 'freedom,' but a fresh label does not eliminate scarcity, incentives, or the bill taxpayers receive. Some public policies genuinely protect rights and solve problems; their outcomes still have to be demonstrated, not declared true by decree. An economy does not improve because a ministry has published a cheerier definition in the Official Gazette.

Saint Augustine offers something more useful than casually diagnosing political opponents with 'psychopathy.' He did not simply teach that every person is 'born good' and then corrupted by power: he wrote of a nature created good yet wounded by sin, and of human will becoming disordered. His political thought also grapples with libido dominandi, the lust to dominate. That warning carries no party card: the temptation to command others can wear red, blue, national colors, or an impeccably pressed suit. Trouble begins when people mistake their certainty for a deed of ownership over someone else's life.

The fairest defense of cross-border political exchange is straightforward: people and parties may talk across borders, provided they respect the law and voters' autonomy. The fair challenge is just as straightforward: anyone denouncing foreign interference should explain why cooperation by their own allies is different. None of this proves a conspiracy run by the São Paulo Forum, requires demonizing every socialist, or pretends rival movements never behave similarly. The bitter joke is selective outrage. In the end, the dispute is no longer about who owns the ball; it is about who wants to own the whistle, the field, the scoreboard—and still send taxpayers the stadium bill.
