Sunday Is Not a Flaw in the Spreadsheet

The religion of endless work forgets that earning a living is part of life, not a deed of ownership over it.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-09

Modern pride has found a peculiar unit of measurement: the number of Sundays sacrificed. Someone spends the week running, reaches Saturday exhausted, then checks a phone on Sunday to see whether anybody has dared to keep working without them. Rest produces guilt; work becomes a public achievement. The calendar has turned into a miniature moral court where an hour of free time requires a written explanation and preferably a productivity certificate.

I have nothing against work. Work can sustain a household, serve others, develop talents and build something lasting. In his 1981 encyclical Laborem Exercens, John Paul II emphasized the dignity of the working person and a vital distinction: work is for the human being, not the human being for work. That should unsettle both employers who regard staff as replaceable components and professionals who can no longer recognize themselves without their job titles.

Catholic tradition does not regard Sunday as a Tuesday with fewer meetings. Paragraphs 2184 and 2185 of the Catechism connect the Lord's Day with worship, rest and time for family, cultural, social and religious life. This is neither an invitation to laziness nor an escape from responsibility. It acknowledges that a human person cannot be contained in a spreadsheet. Someone who knows only how to produce may forget whom all that production was supposed to serve.

Picture a family at the table. The father answers a work message, the mother finishes a task, the son watches a video about becoming rich before twenty, and Grandma asks whether anyone wants more coffee. Nobody hears her. They are all too busy managing the future to notice the present quietly leaving the room. Soon an app will sell a premium family-time package: thirty notification-free minutes, automatic renewal and a cancellation fee for missing one another.

There is a serious objection. Hospitals, transportation, essential services and households dependent on daily income cannot simply shut down every Sunday. The Catechism itself recognizes family needs and important social services as legitimate exceptions. Nor can every worker choose their shifts. That is precisely why rest should not be dismissed as a luxury reserved for those who can afford it, but defended as a human good wherever employers, communities and families can make room for it.

Nor should faith become an excuse to police other people's consciences. The Catholic meaning of Sunday springs from belief in Christ's Resurrection, yet criticism of life reduced to office hours reaches further. A father who never listens to his daughter, a business owner who cannot dine without checking sales and a student who despises themselves for taking an afternoon off need not first agree on theology to see the problem. There is a kind of poverty no bank statement records: owning things while having no time to be with anyone.

Dear reader, work diligently. Study, build a business, do what must be done. But distrust the voice that treats every minute of rest as a sin against efficiency. Attend Mass if that is your faith; sit at the table; visit your parents; listen to your children; pray; contemplate something without a buy button. Sunday is not a flaw in the calendar that productivity has yet to fix. It reminds us that life is larger than the factory, the office and the screen. If even a machine must shut down to avoid burning out, perhaps a human being should not have to apologize for being human.

Sources and further reading

  • Catecismo da Igreja Católica, §§ 2184–2185 — descanso dominical, vida familiar e exceções legítimas
  • João Paulo II, Laborem Exercens (14/09/1981), especialmente §§ 6, 9 e 19
