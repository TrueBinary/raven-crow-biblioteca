Brazilian Senate approves fiscal adjustment bill's main text by 45 votes to 31; four provisions remain

Bill PLP 299/2026 would curb tax breaks and expand spending controls, but has not completed the legislative process.

What happened

On Thursday evening, November 12, 2026, Brazil's Senate approved the main text of supplementary bill PLP 299/2026, a government fiscal adjustment package, by 45 votes to 31. Senator Eduardo Braga (MDB-AM) reported a revised version of the proposal introduced by Jaques Wagner (PT-BA). Four separately challenged provisions remain for a vote expected on November 17, after which the bill must still go to the Chamber of Deputies. The Senate news agency reported that the executive branch estimates an improvement of R$45.1 billion to R$56.1 billion in the 2027 primary fiscal balance, a projection rather than savings already achieved. The package includes reductions in tax benefits, additional budget-blocking powers and changes to transfers under the Kandir Law. Agência Brasil reported a broader R$50 billion to R$60 billion estimate attributed to Planning Minister Bruno Moretti; the figures reflect different estimates and scopes.

The Crow's analysis

The Senate vote is an important legislative step, but it does not amount to final approval or implementation. The proposal would cap inflation-adjusted spending growth at 2% rather than the current 2.5% in years when specified restraint measures apply, and allow further budget freezes to pursue the center of the fiscal target. Changes to tax benefits and state transfers face political resistance. Pending Senate votes and the Chamber of Deputies could still alter the text, while actual fiscal effects will depend on implementation and revenue and spending outcomes.

Counterpoint

The government is celebrating projected savings before the legislative vote is even finished — like counting proceeds from a sale before finding a buyer. Taxpayers deserve a complete accounting of which tax breaks end, which spending actually falls and who bears the costs. Calling this a law already passed would also be premature.
