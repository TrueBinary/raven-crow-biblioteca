Brazil's electoral court fines Facebook R$7.5 million for failing to comply with election orders; deliberate bias not established

Unanimous ruling penalizes delayed and incomplete responses to court orders during the presidential runoff; Meta denies favoring either candidate.

What happened

Brazil's Superior Electoral Court (TSE) unanimously decided on Tuesday evening, November 10, 2026, to fine Facebook Serviços Online do Brasil Ltda., part of Meta, R$7.5 million for failing to comply with court orders concerning the distribution and reach of election-related content during the presidential runoff. The case was brought by Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva's coalition, which alleged unequal treatment of content linked to his campaign and Flávio Bolsonaro's candidacy. The court said the company supplied incomplete information, missed deadlines and submitted material that was difficult to verify, despite taking some corrective steps. The fine consists of three daily penalties of R$500,000 from October 17 to 19 and six of R$1 million from October 20 to 25. Reporting judge Marco Aurélio Bellizze said the record did not establish deliberate action by the company to tilt the election.

The Crow's analysis

The ruling separates noncompliance with judicial orders, which prompted the fine, from intentional political favoritism, which was not established in this proceeding. It highlights the practical difficulty of auditing algorithmic content distribution and artificial intelligence tools during an election campaign. The court found generic assurances of equal treatment and aggregated data insufficient for meaningful oversight. That does not establish deliberate vote manipulation or prove that every reported technical failure targeted one campaign. The debate also involves electoral fairness, platform transparency and freedom of expression.

Counterpoint

Meta's legal representatives denied favoring either candidate and maintained that the company had complied with court orders, attributing identified problems to technical faults that were corrected. The court deemed its response insufficient to avoid a penalty, but the reporting judge explicitly said deliberate electoral interference would require stronger evidence in appropriate proceedings. The ruling is not a finding of election fraud or proof that the result was changed.
