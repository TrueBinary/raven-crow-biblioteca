Santander raises offer for remaining Brazilian stake by 5%, valuing deal at €1.9 billion

The Spanish parent improves its share-exchange terms for roughly 10% of Santander Brasil still held by outside investors; the transaction has not closed.

What happened

Banco Santander announced on November 12, 2026 that it had increased by 5% the share-exchange ratio offered to Santander Brasil minority investors compared with the terms announced on July 30. The Spanish parent aims to acquire the common shares, preferred shares, units and American Depositary Shares (ADSs) it does not already own, representing roughly 10% of the Brazilian subsidiary's capital. Under the revised terms, each Santander Brasil unit or ADS would receive 0.4260 newly issued Banco Santander shares before dividend adjustments, while each common or preferred share would receive 0.2130. Full acceptance would involve issuing around 166 million new shares, or approximately 1.1% of the parent's existing share capital, with maximum consideration estimated at €1.9 billion based on the previous day's closing price. Santander expects to seek approval for the capital increases at an extraordinary shareholders' meeting scheduled for December 15, 2026. The offer remains subject to regulatory and other customary conditions. It is not a completed takeover, nor an acquisition of the entire Brazilian bank, most of which is already controlled by the group.

The Crow's analysis

The revised proposal aims to make the deal more attractive to minority shareholders and simplify the group's ownership structure. Consideration consists of newly issued parent-company shares delivered through Brazilian Depositary Receipts (BDRs) or ADSs, rather than an all-cash payment. Santander projects that the transaction could lift earnings per share by approximately 0.3% from 2029, an estimate subject to market risks. Reuters reports that third-quarter net profit in Brazil fell 15% year on year and provisions rose 23%, highlighting pressure on the subsidiary's performance. The offer is voluntary and does not in itself imply delisting from Brazil's B3 exchange.

Counterpoint

For minority shareholders, the key question is whether the new exchange ratio fairly compensates them, taking account of currency risk, liquidity and the prospects of both banks. The parent argues that investors would gain exposure to a more diversified global group. But receiving parent-company shares is not the same as receiving €1.9 billion in cash: that is the estimated maximum value of the deal, and the outcome depends on participation and approvals. A 5% improvement in exchange terms does not guarantee future share-price gains.
