Israeli strike hits Nasser Hospital in Gaza, killing six including a surgeon

The strike damaged a surgical area in Khan Younis; Israel says it targeted Hamas members, but claims of military use of the hospital have not been independently verified.

What happened

An Israeli airstrike hit a building at Nasser Hospital in Khan Younis, southern Gaza, early on November 12, 2026. Gaza's civil defence reported at least six deaths, including surgeon Rezik Abushira, and about 20 people injured. Reuters and the Associated Press described damage to surgical and other hospital areas, with footage showing rubble, patients being evacuated and rescue workers at the scene. The casualty figures come from local health and civil-defence authorities and may change as further information emerges. Israel's military confirmed the operation, saying it targeted a Hamas commander and other members of the group allegedly hiding in the hospital complex. Israel alleged the facility was being used for military operations, but the claims were not independently substantiated in the reports reviewed. The strike occurred amid a ceasefire negotiated in 2025, which both sides accuse the other of violating.

The Crow's analysis

Nasser Hospital is among the most important medical facilities still operating in southern Gaza. Attacks on healthcare infrastructure compound the difficulty of treating patients amid shortages and widespread damage. International humanitarian law gives hospitals special protection. Allegations of military use do not automatically remove obligations to distinguish civilians from combatants, verify targets, take precautions and avoid disproportionate civilian harm. Determining whether this operation complied with the law requires an independent examination of its aims, methods and consequences. Both Israel's allegations about Hamas activities and Gaza authorities' casualty reports must be clearly attributed.

Counterpoint

If militants were operating inside the hospital, as Israel alleges, that claim requires verifiable evidence rather than a military statement alone. Even when a lawful military target exists, patients, medical workers and civilians remain protected. Conversely, using a hospital as a military base, if established, would itself be a serious violation. The task is not to accept either side's claims on faith, but to demand evidence, investigation and accountability. A ceasefire accompanied by strikes on hospitals highlights the gap between diplomacy and actual civilian safety.
