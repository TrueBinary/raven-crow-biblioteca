BREAKINGJensen Huang steps down as Nvidia CEO; Colette Kress takes over on an interim basis

The cofounder leaves the chipmaker's top job and board amid a governance crisis; the chief financial officer will lead the company as it searches for a successor.

What happened

Jensen Huang stepped down as Nvidia's chief executive on Thursday, November 12, 2026. The US chipmaker is a major supplier of infrastructure used in artificial intelligence. Chief Financial Officer Colette Kress will serve as interim CEO while the company searches for a permanent replacement, according to Reuters and the Associated Press. Huang, who cofounded Nvidia in 1993, is also leaving its board of directors, Reuters reported. The Associated Press also reported the appointment of Harvey C. Jones as board chair. The decision follows allegations concerning Huang's personal relationships and possible national security risks, which remain under investigation. Nvidia previously said it had no reason to believe company secrets or national security had been compromised; public evidence has not established that Huang engaged in espionage. The company's shares fell sharply in premarket trading, according to the news agencies.

The Crow's analysis

Huang's departure ends more than three decades of leadership during which Nvidia grew from a graphics technology company into a central supplier for the global AI industry. Naming the chief financial officer as interim leader may provide administrative continuity, but leaves unanswered who will guide the company's long-term technology strategy. Investors are also weighing corporate governance, internal safeguards and reputational risk. Allegations surrounding the crisis should not be presented as proven findings while the investigations continue.

Counterpoint

The company supplying chips for much of the AI revolution now faces a problem that no GPU can solve: succession and trust. Huang helped build Nvidia into a global powerhouse, but the company's future should not depend on a single leader's image. Serious scrutiny and transparency are essential; declaring guilt before the evidence is not.
