THE BAKERY, THE STAMP AND A PERMIT FOR SUNRISE

When freedom requires authorization, even bread leaves the oven afraid of the government counter.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-09

Picture a bakery opening at six in the morning. Five minutes later an imaginary inspector walks in, not to buy bread but to ask whether the flour has filed a declaration of good intentions. The baker points to the oven; the inspector points to the form. The bread rises, the queue grows, the paperwork piles up—and the only thing forbidden to grow without permission is the business. Here is the inversion: citizens must prove they deserve to work, while public authority assumes it deserves obedience.

This is more than a joke without an address. Brazil's Economic Freedom Law of September 20, 2019 expressly recognizes private individuals' good faith toward the state and calls for subsidiary, exceptional government intervention in economic activity. Article 3 provides, subject to legal conditions, relief from licensing for low-risk activities and a presumption of good faith in certain economic acts. Consider the irony: lawmakers had to write down that someone trying to start a business should not automatically be treated as a suspect. Freedom arrived in the official gazette carrying its own birth certificate.

Before someone claims I want bakeries without hygiene standards or buildings without safety rules, let us distinguish the issues. Inspecting genuine risks is legitimate; demanding a document that prevents none is merely turning caution into ritual. The law preserves environmental and labor protections and sets limits and exceptions. It grants no general permission to harm others. It does raise a question that should haunt every government office: does this requirement protect anyone, or does it merely protect the requirement itself?

The most delicious detail is Article 4: the law instructs the state to avoid regulatory abuse, including increasing transaction costs without demonstrating benefits and manufacturing demand for registries and paperwork. In counter-service language, the government had to tell itself not to invent queues as a hobby. I picture a committee meeting to decide whether another committee is needed. After three meetings it concludes that a working group must investigate the absence of work. Minutes approved, coffee served, citizen still waiting.

There is a moral principle beneath the paperwork. A society of free people should not begin every encounter with public authority by assuming guilt. Business owners can commit fraud, inspectors can abuse power, consumers can suffer harm; that is why rules, evidence, inspections and accountability exist. But the possibility of wrongdoing does not justify treating every initiative as a crime in advance. When the exception becomes the rule, the state stops refereeing and starts playing, selling tickets and charging for complaints—although this time the baker only wanted to bake bread.

A serious objection deserves an answer: poorly designed deregulation can leave consumers and workers exposed, especially where risks are high and enforcement is weak. The answer is neither to abolish oversight nor to multiply it blindly. Define risks clearly, inspect what matters and demand results from those wielding authority. Sound regulation should be able to explain its purpose without hiding behind 'we have always done it this way.' If a rule cannot explain whom it protects, perhaps it protects only its author's position—though that suspicion would require evidence before being leveled at any particular agency.

Dear reader, beware of a country where starting a business feels like a favor bestowed by a clerk and obedience is treated as sufficient qualification to govern. The 2019 law did not abolish bureaucracy, and a right on paper does not prove it is honored on every street corner. But it put a worthy principle into writing: liberty does not originate in a civil servant's benevolent stamp. If an office ever demands a permit for the sun to rise, somebody will fill out the form before opening the window. I would rather open the bakery—and let the smell of fresh bread be the first thing to travel without permission.
