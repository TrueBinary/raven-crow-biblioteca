THE SOCKS COST TWENTY REAIS. YOUR BIOGRAPHY COMES FREE.

A shop wants to sell a product. Why does it need to know the buyer's whole life?

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-22

There is an odd intimacy between Brazilians and the checkbox. To buy a pair of socks, you may be asked for your name, phone number, birthday and permission to receive offers until the end of time. The socks cost twenty reais; your biography comes free. Later, messages arrive from companies you barely remember, and everyone acts as though this were a weather event.

Brazil's General Personal Data Protection Law, Law 13,709 of 2018, makes a point that should be obvious: information about a person does not become the unrestricted property of whoever collected it. Article 18 provides rights of access, correction and, under certain conditions, deletion. The National Data Protection Authority also explains that people may ask with whom their information was shared. It is a right to inspect your digital shadow and see how many strangers are standing on it.

The trouble does not necessarily begin with a hacker. It begins when we accept that a simple purchase should turn into an inquiry into our lives. Businesses may need an address to deliver goods and information to issue tax documents. Fair enough. The questionable leap comes when operational necessity becomes an excuse to know a customer better than their own relatives do.

I will not pretend that every use of data is a conspiracy. Services can improve by remembering preferences, preventing fraud and providing appropriate support. The law itself recognizes several legal bases for processing; consent is not the only one. Nor is there a magic button that erases records a business is legally required to keep: Articles 15 and 16 establish conditions and exceptions. Freedom without responsibility would make a convenient slogan, but a false legal claim.

That is exactly why transparency matters. Anyone requesting information ought to be able to explain, without a forty-page tour of legal jargon, why it is needed and what will be done with it. People should be able to challenge abuse without enrolling in evening classes on digital law. The authority advises contacting the data controller first and keeping records before filing a petition. The right exists; exercising it still demands patience no one added to the shopping cart.

Privacy does not mean hiding from the world or preventing businesses from operating. It means acknowledging that the ability to record our every move is not a moral license to do so. I want to buy socks, not acquire a permanent witness to my existence. If a shop needs to know my soul to sell cotton, perhaps it is in the wrong business.
