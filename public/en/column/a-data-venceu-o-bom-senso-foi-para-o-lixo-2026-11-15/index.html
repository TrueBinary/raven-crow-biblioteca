THE DATE EXPIRED. COMMON SENSE WENT INTO THE TRASH.

Between treating a label like a prophecy and bragging about expired yogurt, common sense is the first casualty.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-15

Dear reader, two types of people worry me when they stand in front of a refrigerator. The first believes a packet of rice turns radioactive the moment the clock passes midnight on the printed date. The second opens an old yogurt, sniffs the lid and instantly awards himself a degree in microbiology. One has surrendered judgment to a label; the other believes his nose has a diploma from Brazil's health regulator, Anvisa. If you expect me to pick a side, you will be disappointed: food safety is not a competition between superstition and bravado.

An expiry date is not poetry written by the manufacturer. In Brazil, Anvisa's Resolution RDC 727/2022 regulates date marking and storage instructions. Their significance depends on the product, its packaging and storage conditions; opening food may change how long it can safely be kept. That is precisely why I will not tell anyone to disregard legally applicable dates or volunteer as a guinea pig to win an argument at the dinner table. Bacteria, unfortunately, seldom respect consumer confidence.

The confusion grows when we import words from abroad without their explanation. In their respective food-labeling systems, the FAO and the European Food Safety Authority distinguish dates associated with quality, such as 'best before,' from safety-related deadlines, such as 'use by.' The FAO cites an estimate that roughly 10% of food waste in the European Union is related to date marking. In the European Union, dear reader. Let us not turn a European estimate into a Brazilian statistic simply because it looks good in a headline. Reality is already taking enough punishment without geography needing to call for help too.

The trouble is how we treat useful information: either we obey it without understanding it, or decide it deserves no trust at all. Between the terrified consumer and the yogurt adventurer lies a third possibility, far less cinematic: read the entire package. Check storage conditions, whether it has been opened, what guidance applies to that kind of food and which safety deadlines must be followed. It is a modest intellectual exercise, I know. It will not produce a heroic video about fighting the system by eating expired food. It is, however, usually more useful.

There is one detail no speech against waste is entitled to sweep under the rug: dangerous microorganisms do not always announce themselves through smell or appearance. EFSA warns of this when explaining 'use by' dates. Meat can look perfectly respectable and still be unsafe. The answer, therefore, is neither to grant blanket immunity to foods past their dates nor to suggest that businesses sell them in breach of food-safety rules. When health guidance protects people, it deserves to be taken seriously. Criticizing waste is not a license for recklessness.

It also helps to look in the mirror before blaming the label alone. Some people shop as though they were provisioning an army, forget half of what they bought, then declare themselves victims of the labeling industry. I understand the anger at waste: behind every item of food stand the people who grew it, transported it, stored it and paid for it. Throwing it away unnecessarily is regrettable. But trying to fix waste by gambling with your health at home merely replaces one mistake with another. Planning purchases, storing food properly and understanding the information is less exciting than a revolution against the calendar. It is also wiser.

I do not want pantry police or a liberation movement for expired rice. I want to know what I am buying, understand what the label says and make responsible choices without turning every meal into a hearing before the Supreme Court of Refrigerators. Industry must give clear information; authorities must protect health; we must do our part. And if any doubt remains, remember this: dates have context, risks have consequences, and good judgment is not measured by how bravely you sniff a yogurt pot. Feel free to debate metaphysics with the rice. Just do not expect bacteria to respect your freedom of opinion.
