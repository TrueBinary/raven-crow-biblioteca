THE DATE EXPIRED. COMMON SENSE WENT INTO THE TRASH.

Between food safety and waste, a label should not replace the judgment of the person shopping.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-15

Picture a perfectly ordinary bag of rice in the pantry. At 11:59 p.m. it is food; at midnight, under a hurried reading of a label, it becomes a suspect in a culinary crime. Nothing snapped, the grain received no court summons, and the saucepan is unchanged. Yet the calendar moves one square and the worried owner prepares a funeral in a garbage bag. Such is the strange power of a date: to inform us about food, or, when misunderstood, to do our thinking for us.

Expiration dates are not superstition, of course. Perishable foods can pose invisible risks; storage, contamination and time matter. In Brazil, Anvisa's RDC 727/2022 governs shelf-life statements and storage instructions, including special conditions after opening. I am not suggesting that anyone disregard a safety deadline, much less gamble with their stomach to win a philosophical argument. The mistake is to treat every date, product and storage condition as identical.

The UN Food and Agriculture Organization distinguishes international labels indicating peak quality from those specifying a use-by deadline. FAO cites a European Commission study estimating that roughly 10% of food waste in the European Union is linked to date marking. Note the geography: this is a European estimate, not a Brazilian statistic conjured onto my dinner plate. The European Food Safety Authority likewise explains why 'use by' requires a different level of caution from 'best before'.

Here lies the paradox. We spent years teaching people to read labels—a real achievement—but have not always taught them how to interpret those labels. The date becomes an oracle, consulted like destiny, while the silent food is denied the chance to submit its technical dossier. Were there a Ministry of Refrigerators, it would surely have an undersecretary to certify the yogurt's sadness and an emergency committee to decide whether a biscuit may still attend breakfast.

There is a serious objection to careless simplification: smell and appearance cannot reveal every microbiological hazard. EFSA warns that food past a use-by date may contain harmful organisms without obvious signs. Producers and retailers also need enforceable rules to protect customers. Agreed. That is precisely why I am not advocating sales outside legal requirements or replacing food safety inspection with household guesswork. I want clear information that distinguishes safety from quality without compromising either.

Wasting food is not merely an accounting mistake. It wastes land, water, transport, energy and human labor. The criticism applies to businesses that discard food without exploring lawful, safe alternatives, to policies that confuse the public, and to consumers like us who buy more than we use. We need no crusade against packaging: comprehensible instructions, sensible shopping and safe redistribution where permitted would be a start. Common sense, remarkably, does not require a ministerial decree to exist.

Back to the bag of rice. It has not acquired eternal immunity because it looks fine, nor has it automatically become a criminal because the clock struck midnight. Foods, labels and storage conditions differ, and those differences deserve respect. A date should help us make safe decisions, not excuse us from thinking. If the label could speak, perhaps it would ask for something modest: read me before you obey me. And please do not appoint a committee to authorize lunch.
