THE EXIT DOOR IS FREEDOM TOO.

An association may pursue legitimate debts. It cannot turn membership into a prison with monthly dues.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-23

Some clubs are hard to leave because you will miss them. Others are hard to leave because Tuesday's meeting lasts longer than the game itself. Either way, the door must open from the inside. An association that welcomes voluntary entry but turns departure into a negotiation with a jailer has replaced community with captivity on a subscription plan.

Brazil's 1988 Constitution deliberately protects two freedoms together. Article 5, item XVII, safeguards lawful association; item XX says nobody may be forced to join or remain in one. The second is not a footnote to the first. It is the sincerity test of every invitation: anyone who truly wants your company accepts that you can leave.

The issue reached Brazil's Supreme Federal Court in distinctly unromantic terms. In Extraordinary Appeal 820,823, Theme 922, the Court considered whether withdrawal could depend on paying a debt linked to a benefit obtained through an association or a penalty. It protected the right to leave without erasing legitimate financial obligations: claims may still be pursued through proper legal channels. The distinction matters. An organization may seek payment of what is owed; it cannot turn membership into handcuffs.

Associations do need rules, resources and commitment. If everyone promises to contribute and disappears when the bill arrives, the shared project becomes a fundraiser where only the organizer brings a wallet. But commitment does not justify confusing debt with belonging. The real questions concern the particular obligation, its origin and its validity, not whether someone must stay enrolled until they develop affection for the board.

What interests me goes beyond the courts. Institutions and movements often praise freedom of choice as evidence of their virtue, yet treat disagreement as personal betrayal. A member who asks about the budget becomes troublesome; one who announces a departure is branded a deserter. When a community survives by preventing goodbyes, perhaps the problem is not the person looking for the exit.

This is no defense of taking benefits and abandoning responsibilities. It is a defense of something more demanding: relationships worth maintaining. A club, union, neighborhood association or other voluntary organization earns legitimacy by persuading people, accounting for its decisions and respecting its members' choices. A padlock can keep a name on the register. It cannot manufacture loyalty. If the only way to keep a member is to stop them leaving, you no longer have a member. You have a hostage with a membership card.
