THE GOVERNMENT GETS THE RIBBON. TAXPAYERS GET THE BILL.

At the inauguration, politicians offer a free project. In the budget, the public discovers who paid for the gift.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-13

Picture the opening of a bridge. There is a ribbon, golden scissors, a podium and a photographer struggling to frame the structure without showing the hole in the sidewalk. The politician announces, as solemnly as if he had discovered electricity: 'We are giving the people this bridge for free!' In the audience, a construction worker who paid taxes throughout the project looks at his colleague. If the bridge is free, perhaps the bills delivered to his home were love letters from the tax office. The master of ceremonies asks for applause. No truck has crossed the bridge yet, but the publicity has already made it across the river.

There is nothing wrong with opening a public project. A bridge can shorten journeys, connect communities and save hours of work. The trick lies in confusing no charge at the entrance with no cost at all. Article 145 of Brazil's Constitution provides for taxes, fees and betterment contributions, each governed by its own rules. The Ministry of Finance explains that revenue from taxes and contributions finances expenditure and investment. Money does not sprout in the ministry garden, although some publicity campaigns suggest Brasília has discovered a tree that grows banknotes.

'Free' is a particularly convenient word because it erases the taxpayer from the picture. Citizens become extras in their own project: they help pay the bill, endure traffic diversions, wait for completion and then watch the ceremony as if they had received a personal gift from the officeholder. The official holds the scissors; those who funded the ribbon do not even get to choose its color. It is a curious reversal: an administrator is celebrated as a private benefactor, and the public becomes a crowd of guests at a party it paid for.

There is a moral distinction between administering collective resources and handing them out as favors. The former requires priorities, contracts, oversight and accountability. The latter asks for gratitude, photographs and preferably a catchy tune. When a project becomes a personal trophy, the question changes from 'What did it cost, how was it contracted and whom does it serve?' to 'Who gets the credit?' Brazil's federal Transparency Portal provides searches of expenditure, contracts, procurement and revenue. It cannot solve every problem, but it reminds us that public money calls for public explanation—even when the inauguration speech ends before the accounting begins.

There is, of course, a serious objection. Not everyone can pay directly for a bridge, a school or emergency care, and collective funding of some services can be justified on economic and social grounds. I agree. Charging tolls on every corner would also be quite a spectacle: buying bread would require hiring an accountant to calculate the journey. The point is not to put a ticket price on everything or deny the real benefits of public investment. It is to ask politicians to speak as managers of other people's resources, not generous uncles opening their own wallets.

This applies to every party. The right can cut ribbons as vainly as the left; a liberal mayor may love a plaque bearing his name as much as an interventionist minister. And legal tax collection is not a blank moral check. An honest debate compares alternatives, maintenance, opportunity costs and outcomes. The bridge may be needed; another project might have been more urgent. Without that conversation, the budget becomes a sticker album: each official collects inaugurations while citizens wonder who will repair the asphalt once the cameras leave.

Back to the ribbon. It falls to the ground, cars pass and the crowd disperses. The bridge will be good if it lasts and serves those who need it, not if the speech produces a flattering headline. And if a pothole appears tomorrow, I hope the same politician returns with the golden scissors—this time to cut red tape rather than another ribbon. Dear reader, a public service can be free at the point of use. It cannot be a financial miracle. When the state says it gave you something for free, thank the workers who financed it and ask for the receipt. The ribbon belongs to the ceremony; the bill is still ours.
