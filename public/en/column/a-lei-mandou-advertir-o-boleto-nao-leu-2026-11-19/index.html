THE LAW SAID WARN. THE INVOICE DIDN'T READ IT.

Article 267 of Brazil's Traffic Code offers a lesson in punishment, proportion and the limits of enforcement.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-19

There is one word in Brazil's Traffic Code that ought to make officials stop and think: 'shall'. Not 'may', and certainly not 'if the department is in a good mood'. Under Article 267, as amended by Law 14,071 of 2020, a written warning replaces a fine for a minor or moderate traffic violation when the offender has committed no other violation in the preceding twelve months. The law stumbled upon a radical notion: you can correct a mistake without reaching first for someone's wallet.

Drivers know the feeling of opening a notice and immediately searching for the amount due. Road safety matters; a moment's carelessness can cost far more than money. Yet the law itself distinguishes conduct and prior records. Someone who slips once is not automatically the same as someone who keeps doing it. Treating them as identical makes about as much sense as expelling a child for forgetting an eraser.

Brazil's Federal Highway Police says it automatically applies this conversion when the statutory conditions are met. That is the crucial point: a warning is not a favor granted to a likable citizen. The previous wording said an authority 'may' issue one; the amended provision says it 'shall'. Sometimes an entire principle of liberty fits inside a change of verb. Some forty-minute political speeches manage to say less.

Of course, highways are no place for romantic experiments in impunity. Serious and very serious offenses, repeated violations, real danger and potential victims do not vanish because a columnist values restraint in punishment. The point is narrower: once lawmakers have decided that a warning is sufficient in specified circumstances, issuing a fine by reflex is not necessarily firmness. It may simply mean failing to follow the rule.

This modest provision reveals a larger issue. The state routinely expects citizens to know the fine print of their obligations. Fair enough. Reciprocity should be equally basic: public authorities must know the limits of their own power to punish. A law requiring drivers to obey signs and officials to obey Article 267 works in both directions. When only one direction counts, that is not traffic education; it is a private lesson in obedience.

Dear reader, this is neither a love letter to fines nor a campaign against enforcement. It is a question about the purpose of punishment when a proportionate way to teach already exists. Drivers answer for their actions; officials answer for what the law allows them to do. There is quiet elegance in a properly issued warning: the mistake is acknowledged, the rule is respected, and nobody pretends every correction needs an invoice. Civilization may begin when power learns to say 'be careful' before it says 'pay up'.
