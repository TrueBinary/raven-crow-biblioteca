THE RIGHT TO COMPLAIN HAS NO TICKET BOOTH.

Brazil's Constitution lets citizens challenge public power without paying fees. Being heard should not require admission.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-23

One of the finest lines in Brazil's Constitution hides in a clause few people quote: you may challenge public power without buying a ticket. Article 5, item XXXIV, guarantees the right to petition public authorities to defend rights or challenge illegality and abuse, free of fees. This is no favor bestowed by an official. It is the right to trouble that official.

There is something deeply civilized about this reversal. For centuries, subjects begged for an audience, protection or mercy. A republic, at least on paper, replaces the bent knee with a citizen who can write: 'This is wrong; examine it.' The recipient may wear a tie, a robe or an ID badge. None makes them the owner of the public ear.

This is not a password that wins every dispute. A petition does not force officials to agree, waive legitimate identification requirements or replace a lawyer where judicial representation is required. Brazil's National Council of Justice explains that citizens can submit complaints to it without a lawyer, but must identify themselves and explain the problem. Opening a door is not the same as promising a favorable decision beyond it.

The most delightful detail is the absence of a fee. If the state could charge to receive complaints against itself, the accused would be selling tickets to the hearing where it faces scrutiny. Brazil's Supreme Federal Court established in Binding Precedent 21 that an advance deposit of money or assets cannot be required to admit an administrative appeal. A defense should not begin at the cashier.

Yet a formal right can be strangled by a practical maze. An unintelligible channel, a reply that ignores the substance or disproportionate demands can empty a complaint of meaning without formally banning it. Necessary procedure must be distinguished from pointless obstruction. Recording facts and addressing the competent body are reasonable responsibilities; turning every submission into an endurance test is a polished way of refusing to listen.

Dear reader, we need not agree on taxes, parties or court decisions to recognize this: public officials owe explanations, and citizens need no permission to demand respect for the law. The Constitution does not promise that every complaint will succeed. It promises something prior to victory: nobody should have to buy the right to be heard. Power that listens only to praise does not govern citizens. It collects an audience.
