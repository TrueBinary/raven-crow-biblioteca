TRUTH IS NOT A BUFFET. AND GOD IS NOT AN OPTIONAL DISH.

Dear reader, if everyone has their own truth, the thief has his too. Trouble starts when his truth meets your wallet.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-10

Dear reader, I have terrible news: truth has been fired. We do not know exactly when it happened, but I suspect it was during a meeting where someone complained that facts were undermining diversity of opinion. In the old days, telling a lie carried the risk of being called a liar. Today you need only recite the magic formula: 'That's my truth.' Done! The lie gets an employee badge, meal vouchers and paid vacation. If tomorrow a man claims there is an elephant in your refrigerator, do not dare contradict him. Perhaps the animal is simply experiencing refrigeration in its own way. And if you open the door and find only a bottle of milk, the problem must be your inability to respect domestic biodiversity.

The intellectual ailment behind this confusion is a particularly convenient form of moral relativism, one whose principles are flexible enough to compete in Olympic gymnastics. The Latin word veritas means truth. A revolutionary discovery, I know. But there is a catch: truth does not change because someone made a pretty Instagram post. Two plus two still equals four, even if an influencer posts a carousel explaining that five is a social construct of traditional mathematics. Reality has the awful habit of existing without asking permission. Perhaps that is why so many people seem to have developed a personal grudge against it.

For us Catholics, the question goes deeper. Jesus Christ said in John 14:6: 'I am the way and the truth and the life.' Notice, dear reader, that Our Lord did not say: 'I am one of several available options. Browse our plans and choose the spirituality that best suits your lifestyle.' Christ did not launch a religious subscription app with a thirty-day free trial and cancellation at any time. Catholic faith holds that God is the ultimate source of truth and goodness. That does not mean someone who does not believe in God cannot recognize truth or do good. It means that, for Christians, truth does not depend on majority opinion. Otherwise, Barabbas might have been voted personality of the year, and the crucifixion would have settled the matter.

What astonishes me is hearing Christians announce, with the confidence of someone who has just discovered an eleventh commandment, that 'you have your truth and I have mine.' My friend, you may have your taste in music, your opinion about football and even the freedom to put ketchup on pizza—although the last of these deserves a canonical investigation. But truth is not an apartment building where each resident chooses a personal law of gravity. If one person says God exists and another says God does not exist, both may be sincere, but contradictory claims do not become simultaneously true just because nobody wants to ruin Sunday lunch. Scripture also teaches that no one can serve two masters (Matthew 6:24), in a passage about God and wealth. It is not an invitation to collect deities like World Cup stickers.

Then we reach one of modern advertising's finest gems: 'You can be anything.' Barbie made this promise famous as encouragement for imagination and career aspirations. Fair enough. The trouble starts when an adult treats a slogan as a fundamental law of the universe. No, dear reader, you cannot literally be anything you want. At thirty-two, you can study medicine, start a business or learn the violin. But you cannot wake up on Wednesday, look in the mirror and announce that you have decided to become a gaming chair. Imagine the family's embarrassment when the man demands to be carried into the living room and complains that nobody respects his calling as furniture. Nor can he simply become an F-35 fighter jet. For some mysterious reason, the air force still insists on technical requirements. Human freedom is extraordinary, but it does not include repealing physics by emotional decree. Recognizing limits does not destroy dreams; it prevents someone from trying to take off from the balcony.

Now picture this scene. You are relaxing at home when a man climbs through the window, takes your television and announces: 'In my truth, this is not theft. It is an alternative experiment in asset circulation.' Naturally, you object. He replies that your outrage shows intolerance toward other concepts of property. He might even apologize that you felt offended while he carried off the television. Of course, this example does not mean every relativist approves of theft or assault; there are serious nonreligious moral arguments against both. But the caricature raises an uncomfortable question: if right and wrong depend exclusively on individual preference, what prevents an aggressor from turning convenience into principle? Fortunately, reality tends to interrupt these philosophical seminars when somebody notices the television is gone.

Christian tradition needs no parliamentary commission to answer that question. In discussing natural law, Thomas Aquinas argues that human reason can recognize fundamental principles of goodness. Truth, goodness and beauty are not just pretty words to decorate the wall of a university that charges forty thousand reais to teach that truth does not exist. They are horizons by which to orient our lives. That is also why the company we keep matters. If you are Christian, cultivate friendships that strengthen your faith, responsibility and character. That does not mean treating everyone who disagrees as an enemy. Christ did not instruct his disciples to build a gated community for people who all agreed. The problem is not talking to someone who differs from you. It is handing your conscience to someone who turns every conviction into merchandise and then charges for the packaging.

And so we arrive at the great contemporary circus: people who relativize truth yet demand that their rights be respected as absolutes; who say everything depends on perspective yet take deep offense when another perspective contradicts theirs; who champion limitless freedom but instantly discover the importance of limits when someone parks in their space. Dear reader, I am not claiming to have all the answers. I am fallible, and that is precisely why I need a truth greater than my convenience. If truth depended entirely on me, I would start by declaring that I am never wrong, my taxes are paid and my bank account has unlimited funds. Unfortunately, the bank manager, the tax authorities and mathematics would insist on disagreeing. Truth does not need my permission to exist. But if certain experts have their way, it will soon need a permit, a notarized signature and three witnesses just to remain true.
