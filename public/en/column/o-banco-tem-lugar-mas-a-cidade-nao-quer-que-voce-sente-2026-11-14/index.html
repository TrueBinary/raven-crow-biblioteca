THE BENCH HAS ROOM. THE CITY JUST DOESN'T WANT YOU TO SIT.

When street furniture becomes a bouncer, the square stops welcoming people and starts deciding who deserves to stay.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-14

Some park benches look as though their designers regard resting as an administrative offence. They have a seat, a backrest, metal legs and a carefully arranged set of dividers to prevent anyone from lying down. The architect was apparently asked to make somewhere to sit and submitted a doctoral thesis on why no one should get too comfortable. The city offers a bench with one hand and uses the other to measure how much humanity can fit on it.

This is more than bad taste. So-called hostile architecture uses objects, materials and shapes to discourage certain ways of occupying urban space. Sometimes the target is someone sleeping rough; sometimes it is young people, older residents or anyone who needs to stop. Dividers can also help people stand up, and that legitimate purpose should not automatically be dismissed. But when a design is chosen to drive bodies away rather than welcome people, the question ceases to be aesthetic: who does the square belong to?

Brazil has legislated on the issue. Law 14,489 of December 21, 2022, known as the Padre Júlio Lancellotti Law, amended the City Statute to prohibit hostile construction techniques in public open spaces when their purpose or effect is to drive away people experiencing homelessness, older people, young people and other groups. It followed Congress's rejection of a total veto by then-president Jair Bolsonaro. The historical irony is exquisite: it took the Chamber of Deputies, the Senate, a veto, an overridden veto and an official publication to remind everyone that public space ought, to some extent, to be usable by the public.

There is a serious objection. Residents and businesses have legitimate reasons to demand safety, cleanliness, accessibility and protection of public facilities. A neglected square with real hazards no longer serves its community either. But the response must distinguish protection from expulsion. You do not solve homelessness by installing an obstacle that prevents someone from lying down; you move that person to the next pavement. The social problem remains intact, merely changing its postal code. It is the urban equivalent of sweeping dirt under a rug and then hiring a photographer to celebrate the rug.

Here is the strange reversal. A city collects taxes to maintain shared spaces, yet sometimes treats the act of remaining there as an abuse. Citizens may walk, shop, work and pay; resting starts to look like a request for special privileges. I am not arguing that anyone may occupy any facility indefinitely, or that softer benches solve homelessness. I am arguing that urban design reveals a moral hierarchy: some people are invited to stay, while others receive silent instructions to disappear.

Christian tradition speaks of human dignity without demanding proof of address first. That does not provide an urban-planning manual or erase genuine dilemmas of safety and budgets. It does impose an uncomfortable standard on governments of every political stripe: a person does not stop being a person because they spoil the photograph of a redevelopment project. Public responsibility requires housing, welfare, health and maintenance policies judged by their outcomes. That is harder than fitting a metal bar, of course. Metal bars do not ask questions, cast votes or show up at public hearings.

Back to the bench. It looks immaculate in the official photograph: fresh paint, clean pavement, an inauguration plaque and enough room for a twenty-minute speech about inclusion. All that is missing is somewhere a person can actually rest. A civilized city need not choose between order and humanity; it needs the competence to pursue both. When street furniture starts pushing away those most in need of shelter, the construction may be finished, but the social project remains incomplete. The bench is occupied — not by a person, but by the fear that someone might sit down.
