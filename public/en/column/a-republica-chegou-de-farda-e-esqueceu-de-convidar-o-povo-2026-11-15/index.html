THE REPUBLIC ARRIVED IN UNIFORM. IT FORGOT TO INVITE THE PEOPLE.

In 1889, Brazil's regime changed before Brazilians were asked. The habit of speaking for the public without listening to it did not end there.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-15

Today is November 15, and an 1889 photograph troubles me more than any parade. It was taken in Bahia, two days after the change of regime in Rio. Outside an imposing building, mounted men, soldiers and onlookers spread across the square. Ordinary people are in the picture, certainly. The question is when anyone asked whether they wanted a new government. Brazil's Republic was born with plenty of uniforms and precious few ballot boxes. For a regime claiming to speak for the people, it was an unusually private opening night.

This is no invention by a nostalgic monarchist. Brazil's Chamber of Deputies itself describes the proclamation of November 15, 1889 as a movement led by Deodoro da Fonseca, backed by military officers, with ordinary people absent from its first decisions. Three days later, republican journalist Aristides Lobo wrote in Diário Popular that the people had watched, 'bestializado'—bewildered and stunned. The word is harsh; its author makes it even more awkward for the triumphalist version of events. He wanted a republic. Imagine throwing a party in your guests' name and discovering, as you cut the cake, that nobody had been invited.

Decree No. 1, issued on November 15, provisionally proclaimed a federal republic and placed the country under a provisional government until a constituent congress could be elected. Consider the paradox: the new political order was declared by decree by those who had just seized power. The monarchy was gone; public participation would have to wait. Brazilians lost their emperor without being asked how their first day without him should begin.

It would be lazy to turn this into a canonization of Pedro II. The Empire was deeply exclusionary too, and slavery had been abolished only in 1888. The Republic opened important institutional paths, including federalism and, eventually, a new constitutional order. But the fact that a change produces lasting institutions does not make its beginnings democratic. A good idea does not receive a free pass through the back door just because somebody has hung the word 'people' over the entrance.

The most interesting lesson crosses centuries without needing to name a modern political party. Some politicians love invoking the people in speeches and treating them as a nuisance when decisions are made. The temptation is not confined to nineteenth-century generals. Any leader who mistakes public authority for ownership of the republic begins to act as though the entire country came furnished with the office. A republic is a public thing. It does not belong to the president, Congress or whoever arrived first at the meeting.

I value living in a republic, but I refuse to pretend its birth was a referendum accompanied by a marching band. That is what historical memory is for: stopping ceremony from swallowing truth. The photograph from Bahia shows people, horses and a crowd outside a building. It genuinely dates from November 17, 1889; it does not show the proclamation in Rio itself. I look at those faces and think about what was missing from the celebration two days earlier: not another raised sword, but someone willing to hear an answer from the people in the square.
