The state wants to manage even your conscience

John Paul II, subsidiarity, and the old temptation to turn helping others into permission to control their lives.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-08

There is a kind of politician who enters public life promising to solve the neighborhood's problems and, five minutes later, is choosing the color of everyone's tablecloth. It begins with a good intention to help and ends with a building regulation specifying when Grandma may serve coffee. Power has an odd instinct to become the building manager: the farther it is from the kitchen, the more confidently it claims to know what is missing from the fridge.

An old word offers an antidote: subsidiarity. Paragraphs 1883–1885 of the Catechism of the Catholic Church explain that a larger social institution should not take over tasks that smaller communities can perform, but should support them when needed. This is neither a campaign slogan nor permission to abandon vulnerable people. It is a question of responsibility and proximity: why hand a distant office what a family, association, school, or neighborhood can handle responsibly?

In 1991, John Paul II addressed this in paragraph 48 of Centesimus Annus. He recognized that an economy needs legal certainty, protected property, stable money, and efficient public services. He also allowed for state intervention in cases involving monopolies and exceptional circumstances. Yet he criticized a welfare state that replaces society, drains its energies, and multiplies bureaucracies. That is more thoughtful than a political chant: neither the total absence of government nor the belief that it must manage every detail of life.

Picture neighbors deciding to repair a public square. They know which tree has fallen, which playground fixture is broken, and which elderly resident needs a bench. Then arrives the Federal Office for Matters Relating to Park Benches, bearing three forms, four stamps, and a public hearing to determine whether sitting counts as recreation. Two years later the square still lacks a bench, but it boasts a governance manual and a deputy department for cushions. The scene is fictional; the danger it mocks is real: mistaking a growing administrative machine for a solved problem.

It would be convenient to conclude that families and private groups can simply handle everything. They cannot. Some children are neglected, older people abandoned, homes are violent, and communities lack resources. Catholic social teaching itself calls for solidarity, justice, and concrete protection. In 2020, Pope Francis explained that when families and local groups cannot meet essential goals, higher institutions should provide the necessary support. Subsidiarity without solidarity becomes an excuse for neglect; solidarity without subsidiarity risks making citizens permanent dependents of a queue.

Nor should small-scale power be romanticized. A neighborhood association leader can be authoritarian, a business owner can abuse their position, and a family can fail badly. Being local does not make anyone holy. What matters is accountable responsibility: decision-makers must answer for consequences, respect rights, and accept correction. The principle asks not only who governs less, but who can serve better without taking away everyone else's initiative.

Ultimately, a free society is not one where nobody helps anyone. It is one where helping does not mean replacing people who can act for themselves. Government can protect the square, provide security, and help those left behind without claiming credit for every act of care. When authority forgets the distinction, even charity gets a case number. And the neighbor who only wanted to offer an elderly woman a bench discovers that kindness has been rejected for lack of a notarized signature.
