BUREAUCRACY DEMANDED A DOCUMENT PROVING THE DOCUMENT EXISTS.

The citizen brings an answer to the counter. The office requests a form authorizing the question.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-13

Picture a citizen at a government counter. He presents proof of identity. The clerk examines it, agrees it is genuine and requests another document certifying that the first one was presented. Could he submit a photograph? No: he needs a signed declaration that the photograph depicts the document. To authenticate the signature, perhaps he should produce identification. This is not an account of an actual appointment but a miniature play about the talent for turning a solution into a prerequisite for itself. Had Kafka experienced some Brazilian queues, he might have abandoned literature to become a paperwork consultant.

The joke gets better when we open the statute books. Brazil's Law 13,726 of 2018 was designed to remove needless formalities between citizens and public authorities. Among other measures, it dispensed with notarized signatures and certified document copies under specified legal conditions, allowing officials to carry out the relevant checks. Then Law 14,129 of 2021 set guidelines for digital government: simplify services, integrate systems, eliminate requirements and make life easier for users. Two laws telling the administrative machine what a grandmother could explain in five seconds: if you already know the answer, stop asking. We have reached the point of publishing official permission not to complicate the obvious.

I am not claiming every public office ignores those laws, or that Brazil's digital services have failed to improve. They have improved, and many tasks no longer require crossing town. My target is the impulse that survives technological change: the belief that citizens exist to feed procedures rather than procedures existing to serve citizens. Putting a useless requirement online does not make it smart. It merely replaces a queue of plastic chairs with a queue of browser tabs. Instead of losing a morning at the counter, a person loses an evening wondering why a website insists on a parent's name matching an ancient record.

Some checks have serious purposes. Public officials handle other people's resources and must prevent fraud, protect personal data and ensure fair treatment. A signature, registration or supporting document can prevent someone from collecting another person's benefit or selling property they do not own. Trouble begins when caution loses all proportion and becomes ritual. Law 13,726 itself considers the social cost of a requirement against the risk it is supposed to address. The aim is not to abolish safeguards but to ask whether each safeguard solves a real problem or merely gives an official the comfort of saying, after disaster strikes, that the form was followed.

The reversal exposes a sickness of power: measuring efficiency by the number of steps completed rather than needs met. Imagine a hospital celebrating ten thousand queue tickets without asking how many patients were treated. Or a school applauding perfectly completed records while students cannot read. Public administration can fall for the same illusion: protocols are marked complete while citizens are still trying to resolve the same problem. The report is a triumph; real life is an attachment that failed to open. If anyone complains, perhaps an interdepartmental committee can investigate why interdepartmental committees take so long.

A government calling itself modern should be embarrassed to ask citizens for information it already lawfully holds and can securely consult. That requires interoperable systems, accountability for data, accessible services for people who struggle with apps and human channels for fixing mistakes. Not everyone owns a new phone, enjoys reliable internet or can negotiate a screen that says only 'unexpected error'. Technology must not become an electronic doorman locking people out of their own homes. When it works, it gives time back to workers, retirees and families. When it fails, it makes the old rubber stamp look strangely comforting.

Dear reader, the crucial question is not how many forms an agency has uploaded. It is how many times someone must prove the same fact to exercise a right or meet an obligation. A citizen is not automatically a suspect, nor an unpaid volunteer in the photocopying department. People fund the administration; they were not born to justify its existence. Return to our man at the counter. Three hours later, he receives a paper confirming that he submitted the paper required to request another paper. He leaves relieved. The office is relieved too: it now has documentary proof that bureaucracy is hard at work. All that remains is to discover for whom.
