BUYING IS EASY. CANCELLING SHOULD NOT BE A MAZE.

A store remembers every click. When it is time to cancel, it sometimes forgets where the exit is.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-20

An online purchase can take four seconds. Regret, curiously, may require a minor career as a private detective. The buy button is enormous, glowing, almost affectionate. The cancel option, when it exists, looks as if someone hid it after losing a bet. The question is not whether shoppers should think before spending. Of course they should. It is why some companies seem to forget the meaning of choice once the payment clears.

Article 49 of Brazil's Consumer Protection Code grants a seven-day withdrawal right for contracts made away from business premises, counted from signing or receipt of the product or service. São Paulo's consumer protection agency explains that online and telephone purchases are covered. This is not a store's generosity, a weekend promotion or a prize for finding a helpful agent. It is a legal safeguard for people buying without the chance to inspect the offer in person.

That safeguard matters all the more in an economy that has learned to sell live. A compelling demonstration, a countdown and an allegedly unrepeatable offer can shrink the distance between wanting and paying. Selling well is no sin: merchants need to earn a living, hosts need to work, and customers remain responsible for their decisions. But persuading someone to buy is morally different from turning a lawful cancellation into an obstacle course.

It would also be dishonest to pretend that every return is painless for sellers. Shipping, inspection, inventory, fraud and the narrow margins of small businesses are real concerns. A withdrawal right does not authorize damaging goods, acting in bad faith or demanding privileges without justification. That is precisely why the rules should be clear on both sides. Legal certainty is not a weapon against commerce; it is the ground on which trustworthy commerce grows.

The trouble is the imbalance of enthusiasm. When taking payment, a company knows your name, your card and perhaps even your favorite packaging color. When you ask to cancel, it suddenly needs to verify that you exist. A free market deserves customers able to say yes, but also able to say no within the law. If a platform can record an order in seconds, expecting it to record a withdrawal without sending the customer on a pilgrimage through support is hardly extravagant.

I do not want the state choosing my purchases or a store choosing my regrets. I want understandable contracts, rules that are honored and responsibility on both sides. The right to withdraw does not replace the good judgment needed to buy; it protects the room to correct a mistake. A purchase may begin with a click. Freedom should not vanish with the next one.
