THE 'ACCEPT ALL' BUTTON AND THE FREEDOM NOT TO CHOOSE

The internet promises to respect your decision—provided you find the reject button before old age catches up.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-10

Dear reader, imagine walking into a pastry shop and hearing the clerk say: 'To buy a coffee, we need to know your habits, track your movements and perhaps tell third parties how often you stare at the cake.' You hesitate. He smiles and offers two options: a giant ACCEPT ALL button and another, hidden behind the counter, requiring a form in triplicate. Behold the miracle of digital freedom: customers may choose anything, provided they choose what the house prefers. The coffee, of course, keeps getting colder.

Online, the scene has a more elegant name: the cookie banner. Not every cookie is intrusive; some support essential site functions, while others measure audiences or help personalize advertising. But there is a moral difference between explaining a choice and designing a trap that nudges visitors into making it unwittingly. The accept button can look like a red carpet; the reject button, an emergency exit behind seven doors. When refusal requires a pilgrimage and acceptance a single tap, the screen has already begun arguing on your behalf.

Here is a detail worth noticing: Brazil's General Data Protection Law, Law No. 13,709 of 2018, requires principles including purpose limitation, necessity and transparency when processing personal data. The Brazilian data protection authority's cookie guidance explains that where consent is the legal basis, it must be freely given, informed and unambiguous, with a genuine opportunity to accept or refuse. This does not establish a universal right to reject all processing essential to a service. It means the word 'consent' must not become a carnival costume disguising the absence of choice.

It is curious how some companies discover free will only when they want to shift responsibility to the consumer. 'You agreed,' they say. Yes, after finding one button the size of a road sign and another hidden like the final clue in a treasure hunt. Imagine applying the same method to a wedding: the officiant asks for consent, YES is projected across the church façade, and NO is available only under Advanced Marriage Settings, version 4.7. That would not be a ceremony; it would be a design trick accompanied by a wedding march.

The counterargument matters. Websites must pay for servers and staff and prevent fraud; advertising and analytics can sustain free services. The law also allows legal bases other than consent in appropriate circumstances, including legitimate interests subject to conditions. Demonizing all data collection would make about as much sense as banning locks because some doors conceal traps. The point is not to demand that the internet run on miracles, but to insist that a choice advertised as free not be sabotaged by the interface presenting it.

There is a small lesson about power here. You do not have to prohibit a decision to influence it; sometimes making one option pleasant and the other unbearable will do. Whoever designs a choice can try to direct behavior without issuing an order. The problem can arise in companies, institutions and governments, and all deserve the same standard. Respect for users is not just a solemn declaration of rights. It is the humble detail of a button that lets them say no without punishment, a maze or humiliation.

Dear reader, perhaps the great technological luxury of our century is neither a faster phone nor artificial intelligence that writes poetry. Perhaps it is finding a website that asks a question and accepts the answer. Brazil's data protection authority did not invent common sense; it merely recorded in administrative language that forced consent is not consent. If the pastry shop ever demands permission to analyze my soul before serving espresso, I will make a radical choice: I will leave. First, though, I must find the 'I don't want coffee' button among forty-seven tabs.
