A VETO IS NOT A THRONE. CONGRESS IS NOT AN EXTRA.

The Constitution gave the president a pen and Parliament the right to challenge its strokes.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-19

There is a peculiar scene in Brazilian politics: the president crosses out part of a bill passed by Congress, and supporters behave as though the pen has settled the matter for eternity. A presidential veto becomes a divine decree; Parliament is reduced to an audience expected to applaud. Someone forgot to tell the Constitution that the republic had become a one-man show.

Article 66 of Brazil's Constitution is less theatrical. The president may veto a bill, in whole or in part, on constitutional or public-interest grounds, and must state the reasons. Deputies and senators may then review the veto in a joint session and override it with an absolute majority in each chamber. The power to say no exists. So does the possibility of hearing no in return. That discomfort is exactly what keeps a pen from becoming a scepter.

This is not an academic quibble. In January 2025, the Senate News Agency reported 55 presidential vetoes awaiting review, the largest backlog since at least 2018. That proves neither that every veto was unjustified nor that one political camp was sabotaging democracy. It reveals something more telling: the Constitution designed a conversation among branches of government, but so many lines can be left unanswered that the agenda starts to resemble an overflowing inbox.

The veto serves a legitimate purpose. A president must be able to block legislation believed to violate the Constitution or harm the public interest. And Congress, frankly, does not become infallible merely because hundreds of lawmakers sit together in an air-conditioned chamber. Overriding every veto out of spite would be as childish as upholding one solely to save the signer's pride. What matters is the substance, the reasons and the consequences.

Trouble begins when each institution falls in love with its own signature. The executive sells the veto as courage; the legislature markets the override as a show of strength. Meanwhile, the citizen expected to obey the law can barely tell which version survived the contest. Politics has a gift for turning mutual oversight into a vanity tournament. And the bill, as usual, is not left in the VIP box.

A republic does not need officials incapable of losing a vote. It needs officials who can explain why they vetoed, upheld or overrode a measure. The president does not own the last word simply by occupying the presidential palace; Congress is not automatically right when it disagrees. If the presidential pen were sovereign, there would be no review. If the parliamentary majority were sacred, there would be no veto. The Constitution distrusted both. Wisely.
