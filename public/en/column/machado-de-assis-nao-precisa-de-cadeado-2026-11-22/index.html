MACHADO DE ASSIS DIED. THE PADLOCK NEED NOT OUTLIVE HIM.

The public domain does not bury an author. It keeps culture from being locked forever in someone's estate.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-22

Machado de Assis died in 1908. More than a century later, he still disproves a remarkably modern notion: that a work needs an owner to remain alive. His novels circulate, are republished, studied and reinterpreted. The man is no longer here to sign contracts. Thankfully, his literature did not have to be buried with him.

Brazil's Copyright Act, Law 9,610 of 1998, provides in Article 41 that an author's economic rights last seventy years, counted from January 1 of the year after death. Machado died in 1908; under that general rule, his works have been in the public domain for decades. This does not mean every recent edition, translation or adaptation can simply be copied. Article 14 protects the creative contribution of translators and adapters without allowing them to monopolize the original.

There is an underappreciated beauty in the passage from private ownership to the commons. Writers work to make a living, certainly, but also to reach people not yet born. Economic rights recognize labor and investment; the public domain recognizes that culture cannot remain locked forever in a family's estate or a company's vault. If some property enthusiasts had their way, even the irony of Brás Cubas would arrive with a subscription fee and automatic renewal.

It is easy to champion unrestricted access once the creator is dead. The harder argument comes from the living: writers, musicians, illustrators and their heirs need to survive, and publishers take genuine risks producing books. Agreed. That is why the law protects economic rights for a long time. But a long term is not eternity. A society that never returned anything to its shared cultural inheritance would eventually be calling an inherited fence an incentive.

One legal subtlety matters: the public domain does not erase authorship. The same law preserves moral rights and, under Article 24, assigns the state responsibility for defending the integrity and attribution of public-domain works. Opening a work to use is therefore not permission to falsify its origin. We can honor Machado without asking a distant descendant for permission to read, stage or publish his original text. Intellectual respect does not require a perpetual toll.

Perhaps this is the true measure of a great writer: when a book no longer depends on an owner and still earns readers. The market can sell fine editions, introductions, covers, translations and editorial work. It need not sell, once again, the right of an 1881 novel to exist. Machado left us fiction full of speaking dead men. Making the living pay rent to the dead just to hear them would be a bit much.
