THE VOTE IS FREE. AS LONG AS YOU SHOW UP.

The ballot protects your choice. The obligation to appear deserves a more candid debate.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-20

Brazil has a peculiar way of celebrating political freedom: it hands citizens a ballot and, with it, an obligation to show up. The choice of candidate is free and the vote is secret, but absence requires an explanation. It is rather like a dinner invitation whose host allows you to order anything, provided you turn up to eat.

Article 14 of Brazil's Constitution makes registration and voting compulsory for literate citizens aged 18 to 70, subject to its stated exceptions. Those who miss an election can provide a justification; those who do not may face a fine. The Superior Electoral Court also explains that missing three consecutive electoral rounds without voting, justification or settlement may result in cancellation of registration. Staying home is not a criminal offense. But neither is it a choice without consequences.

There is a respectable case for compulsion: broad turnout helps prevent only the wealthy, organized or highly motivated from deciding for everyone. Democracy should not be a members-only club, and unequal abstention can weaken representation. That argument deserves more than a joke. Yet it raises an awkward question: does attendance create political judgment? Someone standing before a voting machine may be there out of conviction, habit or fear of an administrative problem. The machine records a vote, not a motive.

In August 2026, a Datafolha survey reported by Folha found that 56% of respondents said they would vote even if it were optional. That is a stated intention, not a reliable forecast of actual turnout. Still, it challenges the notion that everyone votes only because an administrative threat is waiting at the door. Millions may regard voting as a moral duty without wanting the state to make that duty legally compulsory.

Optional voting would not be a miracle cure, either. It would require serious attention to participation among people facing long journeys, exhausting shifts and poor access to information. Making polling accessible, teaching citizens to distinguish proposals from slogans and holding elected officials accountable are less glamorous than speeches about civic virtue, but probably more useful. A democracy that needs people to show up should give them reasons to want to.

I want voting to be taken seriously precisely because the decision belongs to the conscience. Citizens may choose badly, spoil their ballots, vote blank or lose faith in every candidate. None of this makes them children on a school trip whose names must be checked at the gate. A republic deserves voters who turn up willingly and responsibly. If sovereignty comes from the people, we should remember that the people do not belong to the ballot box.
