THE REFRIGERATOR IS HOSTAGE TO A SPARE PART. AND ITS OWNER PAYS THE RANSOM.

The right to repair reveals a contradiction: we buy the appliance, yet cannot always choose how to keep it working.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-12

Picture this: a family's refrigerator stops working on a Friday. The repair technician opens the back, inspects a part no bigger than a coin and delivers the verdict: the whole appliance may have to go. In the kitchen, the milk warms up; in the living room, the family budget freezes. The customer bought a refrigerator, yet discovers that in some markets buying something does not necessarily mean being able to repair it. The manufacturer sells the appliance, while the fate of a tiny part appears to rest with a secret Supreme Council of Screws.

There is a vast difference between innovation and forced disposal. Nobody demands that machines last forever or that every component be manufactured indefinitely. But suspicion is warranted when replacement becomes easier than repair not because repair is impossible, but because access to parts, information or service is restricted. Ownership begins to look peculiar when you can pay for an object and take it home, yet remain dependent on others to exercise a basic freedom: extending its useful life. It is like buying a house only to discover that replacing a doorknob requires the architect's permission and the lock superintendent's signature.

Brazil is not starting from scratch. Article 32 of its Consumer Protection Code requires manufacturers and importers to supply replacement parts while a product is manufactured or imported and for a reasonable period afterward. The rule does not promise eternal availability or free repairs for everything. It does recognize that selling a product and then making its components vanish deserves legal limits. The right exists on paper; the practical question is whether buyers can exercise it without hiring a private detective to find a hinge.

The European Union took another route with Directive 2024/1799, adopted in 2024, which member states were required to transpose by July 31, 2026. It seeks to make repairs more accessible and establishes obligations for categories covered by EU repairability requirements. It is not a magic order to resurrect every toaster, nor does it automatically govern every product sold in Brazil. It answers a straightforward question: when something can be repaired, why should the option feel like a favor bestowed upon its owner? For once, the European bureaucrat has found a useful purpose for a form: telling people the price and timeline before they sell a kidney to buy a new machine.

There is a serious counterargument, and it should not be hidden beneath the workbench. Manufacturers invest in design, safety, research and service networks; stocking parts and performing poor repairs carry real costs and risks. Requiring infinite inventories would raise prices and could penalize innovation. The solution is not to decree that every phone must come apart with a spoon. It is to distinguish proportionate requirements from artificial barriers, transparency from information monopolies, and legitimate safety from commercial excuses. Economic freedom is not permission to promise one thing to customers and deliver a maze of dependencies.

There is also an environmental irony. Corporate and government campaigns love sustainability, especially when it fits on green packaging or in a promotional video featuring slow-motion trees. But environmental responsibility begins with an uncinematic question: can the broken part be replaced? A society that celebrates recycling while making repair difficult risks turning the rubbish bin into a marketing department. The planet gets the speech; the family gets the bill. Meanwhile the technician is still hunting for the component, perhaps assisted by an archaeologist specializing in extinct appliances.

Dear reader, defending the right to repair does not mean declaring war on industry or asking government to manage every screw. It means remembering that ownership, responsibility and competition must survive the receipt. Manufacturers should be free to innovate; customers should know the maintenance conditions and have reasonable choices about caring for what they own. Back in the kitchen, perhaps the refrigerator really does need replacing, and sometimes that will be the best deal. But the decision should follow an honest assessment, not the mysterious disappearance of a part. After all, the appliance was bought to preserve food—not to hold its owner hostage.
