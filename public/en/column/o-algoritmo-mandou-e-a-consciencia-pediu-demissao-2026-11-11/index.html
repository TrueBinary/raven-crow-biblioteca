THE ALGORITHM GAVE THE ORDER. CONSCIENCE RESIGNED.

Technology can recommend a decision. It cannot take the blame instead of the person who made it.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-11

Dear reader, picture the scene: a manager approves a disastrous decision, the company loses money, and he enters the meeting with the serenity of a Tibetan monk. Facing his colleagues, he offers his defense: 'It wasn't me. It was the algorithm.' Wonderful. For centuries humanity invented excuses for its mistakes; now we have found a way to outsource conscience itself. The computer earns no executive salary, attends no board lunches, and yet has been promoted to universal scapegoat. If the losses are large enough, perhaps they will give it a board seat too—provided it signs the minutes with a USB cable.

There is nothing wrong with using machines to spot patterns, organize information or save labor. Artificial intelligence can be an extraordinary tool. Trouble begins when the tool is no longer presented as a tool but as automatic absolution. 'The system recommended it' does not answer who chose the data, set the objective, accepted the risk, ignored the warning or decided to follow the recommendation. A compass points in a direction; it does not take responsibility for the person who walks off a cliff to save five minutes.

The issue is concrete enough that UNESCO's Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, adopted in November 2021, stresses human oversight and says AI systems must not displace ultimate human responsibility. Consider the irony: an international document was needed to remind us that a machine should not become its user's moral alibi. I can imagine an official receiving the recommendation and demanding a committee to investigate whether the previous committee had consulted a chatbot. Technological progress is impressive; the human talent for avoiding one's own signature remains handmade.

Catholic tradition had words for this problem long before anyone taught a machine to finish sentences. In paragraphs 1731–1735, the Catechism of the Catholic Church links freedom, reason, will and responsibility for voluntary acts, while acknowledging that fear, ignorance and other circumstances can diminish culpability. That is wiser than assigning blame wholesale. We are not omnipotent, we cannot control every consequence and we do not know everything. But neither do we become innocent merely by placing a glowing screen between our will and the outcome. Conscience is not an app you uninstall when an inconvenient update arrives.

There is an important counterargument: automated decisions can be hard to understand, and a person may genuinely be misled by an opaque system or required to use it without the power to correct it. Responsibility must follow decision-making power. It would be unjust to blame the employee who pressed the only available button while management designed the process and the company concealed the risks. That is precisely why transparency, human review and clear accountability at each stage matter. You do not fix a mistake by finding a scapegoat with an employee badge—or christening the server Judas Iscariot.

What troubles me, dear reader, is our fascination with a life in which every decision comes with a ready-made excuse. Once, a man said he was following orders. Then he said everybody did it. Now he says the statistical model suggested it. Technology changed; the old desire to escape moral judgment remains fully updated. If an app tells you tomorrow to mistreat your neighbor, the blame does not automatically pass to your phone. And if it recommends a good deed, the credit still involves your choice. Freedom is not never receiving advice; it is refusing to hand the steering wheel of your conscience to something that cannot answer for the crash.

Perhaps the real test of human intelligence is not building a machine that can answer every question, but remaining willing to answer for our own choices. A society that demands explanations from intelligent systems while accepting silence from responsible people has reversed the proper order. Technology can help us think, compare and decide. It cannot confess your sins, make amends on its own or apologize in your place. When the next administrative catastrophe comes with the words 'the algorithm did it,' I propose a revolutionary innovation: invite the person who pressed the button into the room. The computer may recalculate the route; conscience still has no outsourcing option.
