TRANSPARENCY REQUIRES A FLASHLIGHT AND A LAWYER

The government says everything is on display. Citizens only need to find the key to the third basement.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-11

Imagine walking into a government office beneath a huge sign reading TOTAL TRANSPARENCY. You ask to see a contract. The clerk smiles, hands you a flashlight without batteries and points toward a locked door three floors underground. The key, he explains, is available by application; the application form must be requested through another application. Welcome to showroom transparency: the institution claims it has nothing to hide, yet seems to have rented a warehouse just to store the light switches.

In Brazil, administrative openness is not a favor granted by an official in a generous mood. Article 37 of the Constitution names publicity among the principles of public administration. Law 12,527 of 2011, the Access to Information Law, governs requests for information and makes disclosure the rule and secrecy the legally defined exception. It is a simple idea, almost insulting to those who prefer the solemnity of a rubber stamp: public money and government acts must be open to scrutiny by those who foot the bill.

But publishing information is not the same as making it understandable. A portal can offer thousands of files, indecipherable tables, acronyms that sound like satellite names and documents requiring a degree in digital archaeology. A citizen looks for the cost of a procurement and finds a spreadsheet with forty tabs, three spending codes and a PDF displayed sideways. The administration proudly replies that everything is there. Indeed it is. You can also hide a needle in a truckload of needles and claim, with statistical precision, that nobody was denied access to the item.

There is a legitimate objection: governments handle personal data, investigations, sensitive information and complex contracts. Not every document can be released in full, and not every decision fits in a social-media caption. The law itself provides justified protections and restrictions. But complexity is no excuse for permanently obstructing scrutiny. Protecting a citizen's personal data is a duty; protecting a minister's political embarrassment is not a newly discovered fundamental right.

There is a curious reversal in this performance. When officials want to persuade the public, communication is immediate: video, slogan, colorful chart, enormous subtitles and someone pointing at the camera as though they had just discovered the future. When taxpayers ask for the figures behind the publicity, the pilgrimage begins: ticket number, deadline, appeal, password, receipt and perhaps an offering to the god of two-factor authentication. The machine that knows exactly where to find you to collect a tax suddenly needs a cartographic expedition to locate its own explanation.

Real transparency does not require everyone to agree with every expenditure. It requires people to understand what was decided, by whom, on what grounds and at what cost. One citizen may find a project necessary; a neighbor may call it wasteful. That disagreement is healthy when both can see the same records. Without intelligible information, public debate becomes rival fan clubs shouting at a sealed box. And whoever controls the box sells tickets to both sides.

Dear reader, government does not need theatrical lighting to look virtuous in photographs. It needs enough light for its actions to be seen, challenged and corrected. A ruler does not own truth or the treasury; the officeholder is accountable for decisions made on behalf of others. Transparency that works only for people who already know the answer is like a window painted on a wall: attractive on the blueprint, but it lets in no sunlight. And if the office insists on offering a flashlight without batteries, it could at least have the decency not to call the darkness a public service.
