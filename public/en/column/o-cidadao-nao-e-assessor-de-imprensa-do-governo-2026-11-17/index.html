CITIZENS ARE NOT THE GOVERNMENT'S PRESS OFFICERS.

Politicians already have offices and communications teams. Voters need not defend them from every criticism for free.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-17

There is a job Brazilians perform for free, without ever being hired: press officer for their favorite politician. A leader announces a project and supporters applaud. The project runs late and they blame the predecessor. It is completed and they demand a statue. Nobody pays them. They volunteer to defend people who already have offices, communications staff and public budgets.

Social media did not invent this habit, but it gave it a round-the-clock shift. In some circles, criticizing a government decision is treated as betrayal and praising it as virtue. Loyalty to a personality replaces scrutiny of a policy. If a measure works, the signer's political colors are irrelevant. If it fails, a smile for the camera cannot make it succeed. A country that mistakes citizenship for fandom ends up arguing over the score while the stadium burns.

Brazil's 1988 Constitution protects freedom of thought and expression. Article 5, item XXXIV, guarantees the right to petition public authorities against illegality or abuse of power without paying fees. Law 13,460 of 2017 also protects users of public services. Under Article 16, an ombudsman's office must issue a final response within thirty days, extendable once with justification. The design is telling: citizens were not meant to be extras in an official publicity campaign. They have ways to complain, demand explanations and insist on proper treatment.

Criticism is no license to fabricate crimes, spread rumors or treat every public servant as an enemy. Governing involves difficult choices; a poor outcome does not always prove malice or incompetence. But fairness cuts both ways. An officeholder deserves an honest assessment, not advance absolution. The opponent who calls everything a disaster and the ally who calls everything a triumph often do the same thing: excuse reality from the discussion.

I have more respect for someone who voted for a candidate and later demands that a promise be kept than for someone who changes arguments after every press conference to avoid admitting an error. The former understands that a vote temporarily delegates power. The latter seems to imagine owning shares in the cabinet's honor. And here is the bitter irony: politicians can change parties, ministers, rhetoric and priorities, while the loyal fan keeps defending a position the leader abandoned yesterday.

Democracy does not require citizens to love those who govern them. It requires the freedom to replace them, scrutinize them and explain why they are wrong. A president, governor or mayor can deserve respect for the office and credit for a good decision. Worship belongs elsewhere, not inside the state. The next time someone says criticizing the government means rooting against the country, remember: a nation has no press officer. It has citizens.
