PRICES WENT UP. THE SPEECH IS ON SALE.

Inflation can slow without returning a cent to the people buying groceries.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-18

A tomato does not read Central Bank statements. It simply turns up at the market with a new price tag, and the shopper discovers that yesterday's money buys less. The vendor needs no monetary-policy seminar to explain the shock. Pointing at the label will do. Somewhere between the shopping bag and the wallet, inflation stops being a tidy chart and starts speaking plainly.

Brazil's IPCA, calculated by the national statistics agency IBGE, tracks price changes across a basket of goods and services weighted by household spending patterns. It is a serious and necessary measure. But a national average is nobody's actual shopping list. A household spending much of its income on food and rent experiences price changes differently from one that buys more services or can switch products. Saying inflation has slowed may be statistically true without meaning that life has become cheaper.

Here is the confusion worth clearing away: lower inflation usually means prices are rising more slowly, not returning to where they were. If something goes from ten to eleven reais and then to eleven reais and fifty cents, the second increase is smaller, but the item is still dearer. We can welcome disinflation without pretending that workers have got their fifty cents back, let alone the first real. Public rhetoric sometimes sells the brakes as reverse gear.

It would also be convenient, and wrong, to blame Brasília for every price tag. Heavy rain damaging crops, an international energy shock, exchange-rate movements and transport disruptions can all push up particular prices. Not every expensive tomato is evidence of money printing, and not every supermarket increase proves a ministerial conspiracy. What citizens can reasonably demand from government and monetary authorities is less theatrical: sound decisions, understandable explanations and no suggestion that shoppers are imagining their bills.

There is also an inequality that rarely makes for a flattering photograph. People with protected assets, investments and room to wait for a sale have defenses unavailable to workers whose wages run out before the month does. Persistent inflation muddies contracts, makes planning harder and hits those least able to adjust their incomes as quickly as prices change. Price stability is not a fussy economist's hobby. It is a concrete way to respect the time people spend earning a living.

I am not asking Brazil to govern by the price of tomatoes alone. I am asking it to stop governing by the slide deck alone. An index is indispensable for understanding the whole; the monthly shopping trip reminds us who lives inside that whole. When a spokesperson says inflation has improved, fine: explain how much, why and what still hurts. Shoppers may not know the IPCA formula. They know perfectly well when dinner has shrunk.
