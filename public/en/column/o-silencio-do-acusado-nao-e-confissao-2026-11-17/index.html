SILENCE IS NOT A CONFESSION. A MICROPHONE IS NOT A JUDGE.

When someone refuses to speak, the public does not acquire the right to invent what they would have said.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-17

There is a scene that keeps returning to television: someone is under investigation, a microphone is thrust toward their face, and a reporter solemnly announces that the suspect declined to comment. For part of the audience, silence has just confessed. It is a remarkable form of forensic science: not a word was heard, yet everyone claims to know what it meant.

Article 5, item LXIII, of Brazil's Constitution guarantees arrested persons the right to remain silent. Item LVII establishes the presumption of innocence until a criminal conviction becomes final. Article 186 of the Code of Criminal Procedure is more explicit still: silence is not a confession and must not be interpreted against the defense. These rules were not written only for likable, innocent or politically convenient people. A right that depends on the accused person's popularity is a favor with an expiration date.

It is astonishing how easily we trade evidence for impressions. If the person speaks, they are accused of rehearsing a story. If they remain silent, they must be hiding something. If they hire a lawyer, that too becomes suspicious. Counsel, whose job is to ensure that the law is respected, is treated as an accomplice for practicing the profession. In this improvised courtroom, the only acceptable answer is one that confirms the verdict the crowd has already written.

Silence does not prove innocence either. A serious investigation can gather documents, testimony, expert findings and other evidence that supports a charge without the suspect's cooperation. Nor should journalists stop asking questions. The press must question, scrutinize and uncover matters of public interest. The line is crossed when the absence of an answer is sold as a definitive answer. A microphone can record silence; it cannot translate thoughts.

This matters most when the accused is someone we dislike. Defending guarantees for an ally is easy; a fan club can do that. The test comes when the person on the other side is a political opponent, an unpopular business leader or someone accused of a horrifying crime. Due process is not a reward for good behavior. It is the discipline that stops the state from replacing proof with its own wishes. The more serious the allegation, the stronger the evidence we should demand.

I confess I worry less about the person who says 'I won't answer' than about the crowd that answers on their behalf. It invents intentions, assigns motives and distributes guilt, then complains that justice takes too long, as though a trial were merely a commercial break before conviction. Anyone who wants a strong justice system should want strong evidence. The day silence becomes confession is also the day speaking stops being a choice.
