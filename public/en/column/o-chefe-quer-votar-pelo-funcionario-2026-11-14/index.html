THE BOSS WANTS TO VOTE FOR THE EMPLOYEE. NEXT, THE PASSWORD TO THEIR CONSCIENCE.

A company buys hours of work, not the right to choose its workers' candidates.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-14

At Monday's meeting, the boss gathers the staff, projects a sales chart and announces a new company target: electing his preferred candidate. Agreement earns a smile; dissent sends people checking their employment contracts. He already manages inventory, shifts and the coffee machine. Now he wants to manage other people's consciences. Soon human resources may demand a notarized copy of every employee's political thoughts.

The scene is fictional; the conduct it satirizes is not. Electoral harassment at work involves pressure, threats, promised rewards or intimidation aimed at shaping the political choices of people in a professional relationship. Brazil's Federal Prosecution Service explained the issue in March 2026; in August, the Superior Electoral Court and labor institutions expanded their cooperation against it. The question is not which candidate the boss favors. Wages pay for work, not electoral obedience.

On October 31, 2026, Brazil's Labor Ministry reported approximately 4,305 complaints submitted to its dedicated channel over three days. A complaint is not a conviction, and responsible analysis cannot treat every report as a proven crime. But the volume warrants scrutiny. There is an unpleasant irony in a business owner praising free markets while trying to monopolize employees' votes. Competition is wonderful until it reaches the ballot box.

The secret ballot exists precisely because a political choice should not depend on surveillance by someone who controls a job, benefit or promotion. Brazil's Constitution protects freedom of conscience and ballot secrecy; electoral law punishes certain forms of coercion. Public officials who exploit their authority to intimidate subordinates also deserve scrutiny. Voting machines have no time-clock slot, let alone an extension cord to the boardroom.

A distinction matters here. Business owners may hold political views, defend them publicly and discuss economic proposals. Employees may speak, agree or disagree too. Not every awkward conversation constitutes harassment. The line is crossed when hierarchy becomes a threat or professional benefits become conditional on political allegiance. Banning every discussion would merely replace the boss's pressure with an opinion inspector stationed beside the printer.

Anyone who values liberty should recognize it even when someone else makes what they consider the wrong choice. It is easy to defend employees' autonomy when they vote like the owner; harder when they contradict the finance department's forecast. The same standard applies to unions, government offices and organizations that claim to defend democracy. No flag turns blackmail into civic education. Conscience is not an attachment to an ID badge.

Back to the meeting. The boss may still show charts, demand results and discuss the country's direction. He may even believe his opinions will save the economy. He cannot treat the company as a privately owned polling station. At day's end, the worker returns the uniform, shuts down the computer and takes home something absent from the balance sheet: freedom of choice. If the boss asks for the password to their conscience, the answer is simple: work is contracted; votes are not.
