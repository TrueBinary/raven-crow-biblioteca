I BOUGHT THE MACHINE. THEY STILL OWN THE SCREWDRIVER.

The right to repair exposes a strange contradiction: owning an appliance does not always mean being able to keep it alive.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-16

A screwdriver used to be a small instrument of household independence. My grandfather did not need to consult a toaster's terms of service to find out why it had stopped working. Today, with some appliances, an owner can buy the machine, pay for an extended warranty and still run into unavailable parts, designs that resist opening or a repair bill close to the price of a replacement. The screwdriver is still in the drawer. What disappeared was the door.

I am not claiming that every manufacturer deliberately designs products to die young. That would be an easy, lazy accusation. Miniaturized components, hazardous batteries, water resistance and genuine safety requirements can make repairs difficult. Yet one economic question survives all these explanations: if I own an object, why can keeping it working at my own expense depend so heavily on the goodwill of a company I have already paid?

Brazil is not starting from scratch. Article 32 of its Consumer Protection Code requires manufacturers and importers to make replacement parts available while a product is being manufactured or imported and, afterwards, for a reasonable period as provided by law. It does not promise every part for every device forever, or free repairs. It establishes something more modest and rather civilized: selling a product does not automatically end responsibility for making maintenance possible. A right written into law, however, also deserves scrutiny at the repair bench.

In the European Union, Directive 2024/1799 established rules promoting repairs for product categories covered by specific repairability requirements; member states were to apply those rules from July 31, 2026. It is not an order to resurrect every appliance, and it is certainly not Brazilian law. But the debate reveals a curious reversal: for years, novelty was sold as synonymous with progress, while repair acquired the smell of defeat. As if replacing a part were a certificate of poverty and buying another machine a sacrament of modern life.

There is a serious objection, and it cannot be swept beneath the workshop rug: opening equipment can cause accidents, undermine safety and expose personal data. Repairability rules must account for that, along with inventory and development costs. Nor should consumers be forced to repair something they would rather replace. The freedom I defend includes buying something new. I simply cannot see why it should exclude the freedom to keep an older product running when a repair is technically feasible and safe.

Notice the inversion. Markets rightly celebrate consumer sovereignty: people's money decides whether a product deserves to survive. But that sovereignty becomes rather ornamental when the only practical choices are to pay for a whole new machine or stare at a dead one on the table. Not every breakdown needs to become an industrial conspiracy. It is enough to recognize that clear information about parts, manuals and repair conditions may matter just as much as the advertising that persuaded someone to buy.

Perhaps technology's finest image is not a product launch beneath spotlights, but two people bent over an open appliance, trying to bring it back to life. There is intelligence there, patience and respect for money somebody worked to earn. Dear reader, I do not want the state choosing my toaster's brand, or the factory choosing the day I must bury it. If the object can still be useful, at least let its owner try to save it. A society capable of engineering wonders yet unable to replace a screw might want to reread the manual.
