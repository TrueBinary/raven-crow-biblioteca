THE SECRET IS PUBLIC. THE KEY IS STILL IN THE OFFICE.

Fifteen years after Brazil's Access to Information Law, public receipts can still be treated like private property.

The Crow Investigator · Raven Crow · 2026-11-18

On November 18, 2011, Brazil enacted a law that ought to make any administration fond of drawn curtains uncomfortable. The Access to Information Law made disclosure the rule and secrecy the exception. Fifteen years later, the sentence still looks splendid on paper. But paper does not open drawers. There is an enormous difference between a government that publishes a ribbon-cutting photograph and one that releases the spreadsheet showing what the ceremony cost.

This is not a demand for ministers to reveal their phone passwords or hospitals to expose patients' medical records. The law itself protects personal information and permits legally justified restrictions. But public contracts, expenditures, administrative decisions and the criteria for distributing public resources concern the people footing the bill. Disclosure is not a gift bestowed by an official in a generous mood. It is what makes scrutiny of decisions taken in the public's name possible.

Article 11 requires immediate access when information is available. Otherwise, the authority generally has twenty days to respond, extendable by ten with a justification. Notice what the law sets: a deadline for the administration to answer, not a deadline for the citizen to forget. Yet someone trying to find out where the money went must master protocols, appeals and reviewing authorities. The government knows its budget; the taxpayer has to learn to hunt down their own change.

One dispute illustrates the problem better than a campaign speech. In August 2026, Brazil's Office of the Comptroller General ordered individualized disclosure of data on legal fees awarded to members of the Office of the Attorney General. The latter raised privacy concerns and technical difficulties in producing the requested format. In October, the nonprofit Transparência Brasil again complained that the information had not been delivered directly. The dispute is documented; it does not, by itself, prove a crime or deliberate concealment. But it shows how the fight over information can continue even after the right of access has been recognized.

Protecting personal data and avoiding the disclosure of information the law requires to be safeguarded is perfectly reasonable. Turning transparency into state-sponsored voyeurism would be irresponsible. But that legitimate concern loses force when privacy becomes a blanket excuse to prevent scrutiny of public payments. Protecting someone's home address is one thing. Preventing the public from seeing how publicly sourced money was distributed is another. Conflating them is a great favor to opacity.

My suspicion has no party affiliation. Secrecy that suits today's administration will suit tomorrow's just as well; only the nameplate changes. It is peculiar how often officials demand absolute trust precisely when they refuse to show the receipt. If the information belongs to the public, those in power should not behave as owners of the key. After all, taxpayers already paid for the building, the lock and the salary of the person saying the drawer must stay shut.
