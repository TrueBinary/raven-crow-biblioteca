Brazil's 13.75% Selic rate versus 24.4% corporate borrowing costswhy the figures differ

November's benchmark rate contrasts with September's average interest rate on unrestricted corporate credit; lending spreads, risk and fees help explain the gap.

What happened

Brazil's Monetary Policy Committee (Copom) kept the Selic benchmark rate at 13.75% per year on November 4, 2026, pausing a series of cuts. Central Bank credit statistics published on October 29 show that the average interest rate on unrestricted credit to businesses reached 24.4% per year in September, up 0.4 percentage points from August. The gap is 10.65 percentage points, but these figures measure different things: Selic is a monetary policy benchmark, while 24.4% is an average across business lending products and conditions. The Central Bank also reported a 32.4% annual average for all new credit contracts in September, including households and businesses; that figure is not a business-only borrowing rate.

The Crow's analysis

The Selic influences funding costs and financial conditions but is not the final rate quoted to a company. Loan pricing reflects default risk, administration, taxes, capital requirements, maturities, collateral and bank margins. The average rate for unrestricted corporate credit rose from 24.0% to 24.4% per year in September; the Central Bank highlighted a 1.4-percentage-point increase in working-capital loans with maturities above 365 days. Borrowing to finance inventory, equipment or cash flow can therefore carry substantial costs. Companies should compare the total effective cost of each offer, including interest and applicable charges. The published average does not mean every business pays 24.4%, or that a particular borrower contracted a loan at that rate.

Counterpoint

The comparison does not show that the government or Central Bank concealed the actual cost of corporate borrowing: both indicators are published and serve different purposes. Lenders cite risk and intermediation costs as reasons for spreads, while critics highlight banking competition, market concentration and fees. Monetary policy aims to contain inflation, and its effect on lending rates may be delayed. Directed lending and collateral-backed products can also carry different rates.
